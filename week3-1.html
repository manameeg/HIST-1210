Buddhism

A faith born from Hindu roots that charted a "Middle Way" between asceticism and worldly life
Part One

The Buddha and the Middle Way

Buddhism begins with a single figure: Gautama Buddha, who lived roughly between 550 and 450 BCE. Rather than choosing between the extremes of harsh self-denial and ordinary worldly life, the Buddha pursued what he called the "Middle Way" — a path of disciplined moderation.

According to tradition, Gautama gained enlightenment while sitting under a pipal tree at Gaya in the region of Bihar. In Buddhist symbolism, the pipal tree is associated with the Buddha's birth and awakening, while the stupa — a domed monument — is associated with his death. He is believed to have died at about the age of 80.

Three Concepts Inherited from Hinduism

Buddhism did not emerge from nothing. Three of its foundational ideas came directly from the Hindu tradition that preceded it:

Samsara — the cycle of transmigration, the endless round of birth, death, and rebirth that all living beings are caught in.

Karma — the moral law of cause and effect. One's deeds in this life shape the circumstances of the next. The word itself simply means "deeds."

Nirvana — liberation or release from the cycle of rebirth. This is the Buddhist equivalent of the Hindu concept of moksha, though the two traditions understand the nature of that liberation differently.

Think About It

Buddhism shares its core vocabulary — samsara, karma, liberation — with Hinduism. What does it mean for a new religious movement to borrow its parent tradition's framework while fundamentally reinterpreting its conclusions?

Part Two

The Four Noble Truths

The core of the Buddha's teaching is compressed into four propositions, known as the Four Noble Truths:

First: Life is suffering (dukh).

Second: Suffering is caused by desire.

Third: End desire and you end suffering.

Fourth: The way to end suffering is through the Eightfold Path.

Notice the structure: diagnosis, cause, prognosis, treatment. The Buddha is often compared to a physician — not prescribing belief, but identifying a problem and offering a method.

The Eightfold Path

The Fourth Noble Truth points to a practical discipline — eight principles for living that, taken together, lead toward the cessation of suffering:

Right views. Right aspirations. Right speech. Right conduct. Right livelihood. Right effort. Right mindfulness. Right consciousness.

These are not commandments handed down by a god. They are practices — habits of attention, behavior, and thought that the Buddha argued any person could cultivate. The path is demanding but democratic: it requires no priest, no sacrifice, no ritual intermediary.

This was a direct challenge to the Vedic Brahmanical system that dominated South Asian religious life. In that system, access to the sacred required Brahman priests who performed elaborate rituals and sacrifices on behalf of others. The Four Noble Truths and the Eightfold Path bypassed all of this. They offered a diagnosis of suffering and a practical method for ending it that any individual could follow — regardless of birth, caste, or access to a priestly class. Where the Brahmanical system made spiritual progress dependent on ritual intermediaries, Buddhism made it a matter of personal discipline.

This accessibility had enormous consequences. Because the Four Noble Truths required no priesthood and no ritual infrastructure, Buddhism was portable in a way that Brahmanical Hinduism was not. It could be taught, learned, and practiced anywhere — which helps explain why Buddhism eventually spread far beyond India, across Central Asia, East Asia, and Southeast Asia, while Brahmanical Hinduism remained more closely tied to the social structures of the subcontinent.

The Eightfold Path requires no priestly intermediary and no sacrificial ritual. How does this contrast with the Vedic Brahmanical system you studied in Week 2? What kinds of people might have found this alternative appealing — and why?

Part Three

The Sangham: Buddhist Community and Monastic Life

Buddhism's institutional structure was monastic. The basic unit of organization was the sangham — a community or meeting of monks. But monasteries were not free-floating spiritual retreats. They required money, justification, and ideology to sustain themselves.

Different sangham denominations existed, and monks from various communities would gather during the rainy season. Monasteries were largely self-enclosed worlds, and monastic councils held the authority to decide what counted as canonical scripture. Near the city of Patna, a university for Buddhist studies was established — one of the earliest formal centers of religious learning in South Asia.

Buddhism also embraced pilgrimage — travel to places where the Buddha had lived and taught. Rather than worshipping images of the Buddha himself (at least in the early centuries), followers venerated symbols: the lotus, the pipal tree, the umbrella, the stupa, and the empty throne.

Think About It

The sangham needed money, justification, and ideology. What does it tell us about the relationship between religion and material resources that even a faith built on renouncing desire required an economic infrastructure to survive?

Part Four

Two Branches of Buddhism

As Buddhism spread, it divided into two major branches, each with a distinct character:

Theravada Buddhism ("Way of the Elders") stayed closer to what practitioners considered the original teachings. It emphasized worship of the historic Buddha only, held up the arhat — the perfected disciple — as the ideal, and stressed monastic lifestyle and individual self-effort. Simple and austere, Theravada Buddhism remains popular today in Burma and Thailand.

Mahayana Buddhism ("Greater Vehicle") evolved a larger, more elaborate system. It introduced a wide number of deities who could be approached by ordinary people, stressed lay life over monasticism, and centered on the figure of the bodhisattva — a being destined to become a Buddha who delays final liberation to help others. Mahayana Buddhism featured complex rituals and devotional practices, and it spread broadly across Central and East Asia.

Buddhism and Jainism: Parallel Paths

Buddhism and Jainism emerged in the same region (modern Bihar), in the same era, and in opposition to many of the same things. The parallels are striking: both founders came from royal families. Both were non-Brahmans who were critical of the Brahmanical establishment. Both rejected Vedic sacrifice, animal sacrifice, and the varna system. Both used non-Sanskritic languages. Both developed missionary tendencies.

Yet their trajectories diverged. Buddhism spread far beyond India — across Central Asia, East Asia, and Southeast Asia — but eventually declined and nearly disappeared within the subcontinent itself. Jainism, by contrast, never spread beyond India, but it never died there either.

Think About It

Two faiths born in the same place, at the same time, rejecting many of the same things — yet one spread globally and faded at home, while the other stayed local and survived. What might account for such different outcomes?

Key Takeaways
  • Buddhism inherited samsara, karma, and the idea of liberation from Hinduism but reinterpreted each concept.
  • The Four Noble Truths diagnose suffering and prescribe the Eightfold Path as a practical, non-ritualistic remedy.
  • The sangham provided Buddhism's institutional backbone — monastic, self-governing, and dependent on material support.
  • Theravada and Mahayana represent two fundamentally different visions of Buddhist practice: austere self-effort versus devotional community.
  • Buddhism and Jainism share deep structural parallels, but their geographic fates were opposite.
