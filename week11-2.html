HIST 1210 · Week 11.2

Akbar and Jahangir

How a twelve-year-old heir transformed the Mughal Empire — and what happened when his son inherited a world shaped by tolerance.

Part 1

Akbar Takes Power

When Akbar inherited the throne in 1556, he was just twelve years old. The young emperor was looked after by Bairam Khan, a powerful regent — but this arrangement immediately created tension. Bairam Khan was a Shia Muslim from Persia, while Akbar's closest associates were Turani Sunnis. The religious fault line ran straight through the court. Bairam Khan was eventually sent into exile on a pilgrimage to Mecca, but he was killed en route — a sign of the deadly stakes of Mughal court politics.

From 1560 to 1571, Akbar stayed at Agra, organizing the empire into four great departments: financial, military, household, and religious affairs. The emperor was, in a real sense, the capital itself — wherever he was, the empire was. Akbar used four different capitals over the course of his reign: Agra, Fatehpur Sikri, Lahore, and then Agra again.

Almost immediately, Akbar faced challenges to his authority. A Rajput revolt broke out in 1561, followed by an Uzbek revolt in 1564. The growing empire required more nobles — more amirs — to govern it, but Akbar did not want to draw them all from Central Asia. Instead, he turned to a strategy that would define his reign: recruiting locally.

Key Context

Fatehpur Sikri, constructed in the 1560s, was laid out like a military camp — with the mosque and the tomb of the Sufi saint Shaikh Salim Chishti at its center. The city represents a transition from the Mughal tradition of ruling from a "camp" to governing from a permanent urban capital.

Part 2

The Mughal-Rajput Alliance and the Mansab System

Akbar's most consequential political innovation was his alliance with the Hindu Rajput rulers. He recruited Rajputs directly into the Mughal administration and cemented their loyalty through marriage alliances. Under this arrangement, new Rajput rajas gave peshkush (tribute) to the emperor and received robes of honor in return. The alliance benefited both sides: the Mughals won the continued loyalty of powerful local rulers, and the Rajputs gained access to a broader sphere of influence than they could have achieved on their own.

To manage this growing network of nobles, Akbar developed a system of honorific ranks called mansab. A person who held such a rank was called a mansabdar. Each mansabdar had two rankings. The first was his zat, a personal rank that could be increased or decreased at the emperor's pleasure. The second was his suwar, which denoted how many troops the mansabdar commanded. This system allowed Akbar to maintain a large army without crushing the empire's budget — nobles were personally responsible for the upkeep of their troops, while the emperor controlled their status and advancement.

Think About It

The mansab system tied military power directly to imperial favor. How does this compare to how other empires you have studied — the Han, the Tang, the Mongols — managed the problem of keeping powerful nobles loyal?

Part 3

Akbar's Religious Revolution

From his base at Fatehpur Sikri, Akbar expanded the empire toward Gujarat and the sea, which would allow access to maritime trade. His victory at Gujarat was the highpoint of his military career. He went on to suppress Bihar and Bengal, and by the 1580s, all major regions of northern India were under Mughal control.

But Akbar's most dramatic transformation was religious, not military. Several influences were at work. The mystic poet Kabir had opened up a spiritual space where both Ram and Rahim — Hindu and Islamic names for God — could be worshipped together. The arrival of Sikhism, founded by Guru Nanak, represented what the notes call an "avowedly synthetic movement" that drew from both traditions. Akbar moved away from devout Islam toward a personal worship of sun and light.

The consequences were sweeping. In 1578, Akbar reformed the position of the ulema (Islamic scholars) within the empire. In 1579, he repealed the jiziya — the tax levied on non-Muslims — and declared himself the ultimate judge in religious matters. By 1580, Akbar's religious enthusiasm for Islam had clearly waned: he no longer sent the annual caravan to Mecca. He began worshipping the sun and encouraged his mansabdars to join him, binding the nobility together through a shared spiritual practice that was distinctly his own.

In 1585, Akbar moved to Lahore after his half-brother died at Kabul, and eventually returned to Agra — better situated for campaigns against the Deccan. Fatehpur Sikri was also running out of water. Five sultanates in the Deccan remained to be conquered: Khandesh, Ahmadnagar, Berar, Bijapur, and Golconda. By the time Akbar died in 1605, Berar (1586), Ahmadnagar (1600), and Khandesh (1601) had all fallen.

Key Context

Akbar's repeal of the jiziya was extraordinary for its time. The tax on non-Muslims had been a standard feature of Islamic governance across the medieval world. By eliminating it and declaring himself the supreme arbiter of religious disputes, Akbar was not merely being tolerant — he was redefining the relationship between political authority and religious law.

Part 4

Jahangir: The Seizer of the World

On October 24, 1605, Jahangir took the throne. Born Salim, he took the name Jahangir — "Seizer of the World" — partly to avoid confusion with the Ottoman Sultan Selim II. Jahangir is known to us through two unusually vivid sources: his own diary, and his extensive use of court painters.

The first seventeen years of Jahangir's reign were largely peaceful, with one notable exception: the disobedience of his eldest son, Khusrau, in 1607. Jahangir suppressed the rebellion and eventually had Khusrau blinded to prevent further trouble. The punishment of Khusrau's supporters was characteristically grim: two men were sewn into fresh ox and donkey skins and marched around in the sun. One died from the constriction.

Jahangir married Nur Mahal — "Light of the Palace" — who later became known as Nur Jahan, "Light of the World." She would become one of the most powerful women in Mughal history. Jahangir's other notable son was Khurram, four years younger than Khusrau. Khurram was talented and ambitious. He married Arjumand Banu, who would later become famous as Mumtaz Mahal — "Chosen One of the Palace." When Khurram returned from a successful campaign in the Deccan, Jahangir honored him with the title "Shah Jahan" — Emperor of the World.

Think About It

Mughal succession was often violent — fathers blinding sons, brothers killing brothers. Why do you think the Mughals did not develop a fixed rule of succession (such as primogeniture)? What were the advantages and dangers of leaving succession open?

Part 5

Europeans at Court and the Seeds of Conflict

During Jahangir's reign, the first significant English presence appeared at the Mughal court. Sir Thomas Roe, sent by King James I to secure a trade agreement for the East India Company, arrived at Surat in 1615. (He was preceded by William Hawkins, of whose diary only a fragment survives.) Roe met Jahangir's son Parwiz first and was forced to lean on a pillar — he was not allowed to sit or stand with the prince. He gave Parwiz some bottles of alcohol on the promise of a later audience, but Parwiz simply got drunk.

Roe met Jahangir himself at Ajmer on December 23, 1615. He brought gifts, but complained in letters to the East India Company that many had spoiled in transit — rotten leather, ruined mirrors. Jahangir, for his part, was most impressed by the English paintings that Roe presented. The emperor was a connoisseur of miniature painting, valuing them both as mementos and as jewel-like objects. Jahangir introduced the halo as a symbol of the emperor in Mughal painting. Roe left India in February 1619 without securing a treaty.

The final years of Jahangir's reign were consumed by family conflict. By 1620, trouble had broken out with Shah Jahan, who was ordered back to the Deccan. Nur Jahan had begun working against Shah Jahan's interests at court. Shah Jahan took Khusrau with him on campaign but had him killed in August 1621. When Jahangir asked Shah Jahan for troops to fight Shah Abbas of Persia over Kandahar, Shah Jahan refused unless granted complete control of the campaign. He was pursued until 1625, when the two sides reached a stalemate and Shah Jahan sued for peace. His two young sons — Dara Shukoh and Aurangzeb — were sent to live with Nur Jahan as a guarantee of his submission.

Jahangir, increasingly ill, traveled to Kashmir and the gardens he had built there. He died on October 28, 1627.

Key Takeaways

  • Akbar transformed the Mughal Empire through the Rajput alliance and the mansab ranking system, binding diverse elites into a single administrative structure.
  • Akbar's religious reforms — repealing the jiziya, promoting sun worship, declaring himself supreme religious judge — broke sharply from Islamic orthodoxy.
  • Jahangir's reign saw the first significant English diplomatic presence at the Mughal court, though Sir Thomas Roe left without a trade treaty.
  • Nur Jahan wielded extraordinary political influence during Jahangir's reign, shaping succession politics from within the court.
  • Mughal succession remained violent and contested — the conflict between Jahangir and Shah Jahan foreshadowed the even bloodier wars of succession to come.
