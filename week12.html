Shah Jahan and Aurangzeb

The emperor who built the Taj Mahal — and the son who imprisoned him and reversed his legacy.

Part 1

Shah Jahan Takes the Throne

On January 24, 1628, Shah Jahan mounted the throne. His wife, Arjumand Banu — better known as Mumtaz Mahal, "Chosen One of the Palace" — had been at his side through the turbulent years of rebellion against his father Jahangir. Shah Jahan's early reign was relatively peaceful. By 1629, he set off for the Deccan, where he suppressed the kingdoms of Ahmadnagar and Bijapur.

Mumtaz Mahal had accompanied Shah Jahan on campaign, but she died in childbirth on June 7, 1631. Of the couple's fourteen children, four sons and three daughters survived. Among the sons, two would define the empire's future: Dara Shukoh, Shah Jahan's favorite, and Aurangzeb, who would ultimately seize the throne by force.

The monument Shah Jahan built in Mumtaz's memory would become the most recognized building in the world. The Taj Mahal was a synthesis of multiple architectural traditions. From Babur and Kabul came the square, divided water-coursed gardens. From Akbar came the minarets and white marble inlay. From Persia came the dome — though the swollen dome of the Taj is unique to India. Work began in 1632, and most of the construction was complete by 1648.

Key Context

The Taj Mahal is often described as a masterpiece of a single tradition, but it was actually a synthesis of Central Asian, Persian, and Indian architectural elements — reflecting the Mughal Empire's position at the crossroads of multiple cultures. The name "Taj Mahal" itself is an abbreviation of Mumtaz Mahal, meaning "Chosen One."

Part 2

The Builder Emperor

Shah Jahan was not content with a single monument. He undertook a series of ambitious building projects that reshaped the physical landscape of Mughal power. He built the Grand Trunk Road from Lahore to Agra, and constructed marble palaces at Delhi, Agra, and Lahore.

In 1648, Shah Jahan transferred the capital to Delhi, founding a new city called Shah Jahanabad — literally, the "Abode of Shah Jahan." For his new capital, he created the Peacock Throne, an extravagant jeweled seat placed in his diwan-i-am, the hall of public audience at Delhi.

A glimpse into Shah Jahan's daily routine reveals the rhythms of Mughal imperial life. The emperor woke before dawn for ablutions and morning prayer at the Mina Masjid. After sunrise, he appeared at the jharoka-i-darshan — a balcony viewing for the public. From there he proceeded to the diwan-i-am for about two hours of public business, followed by the diwan-i-khas, the hall of private audience. Around noon he retired to the harem for a meal and a brief nap. After his nap came business of the harem, then public prayer at three o'clock, followed by more business at the diwan-i-am. Dinner came by 7:30, and the emperor was read to from the Babur-nama — the memoirs of the dynasty's founder — before bed at ten.

Think About It

Shah Jahan's daily schedule was structured around both prayer and public display. What does this combination tell us about how Mughal emperors maintained their authority? How does this compare to the daily routines of rulers in other cultures you have studied?

Part 3

A Turn Toward Orthodoxy

After Mumtaz Mahal's death, Shah Jahan came increasingly under the influence of the ulema — the Islamic religious scholars. This was a notable shift from the religious openness that had characterized Akbar's reign. Shah Jahan forbade wearing the image of the emperor in miniature painting, ended the practice of prostration before the emperor, and ordered all newly constructed Hindu temples destroyed.

The module's source notes describe this behavior as "an anomaly in an otherwise tolerant reign." Shah Jahan was not anti-Hindu in any comprehensive sense — the notes even describe him as being sympathetic toward Hindu traditions in other contexts. But the shift toward the ulema represented a meaningful change in the relationship between the Mughal throne and Islamic orthodoxy, one that would accelerate dramatically under his son Aurangzeb.

Shah Jahan also clashed with European powers. In the 1630s, he sent his general to attack the Portuguese settlement at Hooghly. The town was sacked, and the survivors were marched back to Agra, where they were enslaved. This was a stark assertion of Mughal authority over a European trading presence that had operated with relative freedom under earlier emperors.

Two military failures marked the decline of Shah Jahan's reign: an unsuccessful attempt to retake Samarkand — the ancestral Timurid capital in Central Asia — and a failed campaign to recapture Kandahar from the Persians. By 1657, Shah Jahan was ill and retired to Agra, leaving his eldest son Dara Shukoh in charge at Delhi.

Key Context

Aurangzeb had been appointed viceroy of the Deccan by 1637. While Shah Jahan kept his favorite son Dara Shukoh close at Agra, Aurangzeb spent years far from court learning the trades of war and leadership — an education that would prove decisive when the brothers finally fought for the throne.

Part 4

The War of Succession

When Shah Jahan fell ill in 1657, Dara Shukoh was left in charge at Delhi. But Shah Jahan's three other sons — including Aurangzeb — immediately made plans to oust Dara and seize the throne for themselves. A war of succession broke out among the brothers.

Aurangzeb proved victorious. He had spent decades in the Deccan, far from the comforts of the imperial court, learning the practical skills of military command and provincial governance. Dara Shukoh, by contrast, had been groomed at court — close to his father but distant from the realities of warfare.

On July 21, 1658, Aurangzeb proclaimed himself emperor. His first acts were brutal and decisive. He imprisoned his father, Shah Jahan, who would spend the remaining years of his life confined at Agra — legend holds that he could see the Taj Mahal from his prison window. Aurangzeb then had Dara Shukoh killed, eliminating the brother who had been Shah Jahan's chosen heir.

Think About It

The Mughal Empire had no fixed rule of succession. Dara Shukoh was Shah Jahan's favorite and designated heir, yet Aurangzeb — the more experienced military commander — ultimately won the throne. What does this pattern suggest about what actually determined power in the Mughal system, regardless of an emperor's wishes?

Part 5

Aurangzeb and the Empire's New Direction

Aurangzeb's reign, which lasted from 1658 to 1707, marked a sharp departure from the religious policies of Akbar and, to a lesser extent, of Shah Jahan. Where Akbar had repealed the jiziya and moved toward a syncretic spiritual vision, Aurangzeb began a program of reform rooted in strict adherence to Islamic principles.

These reforms came at a cost. The return to orthodoxy strained the empire's finances and alienated segments of the population that had benefited from the more inclusive policies of earlier emperors. When the reforms began to bankrupt the treasury, Aurangzeb reinstated the jiziya — the tax on non-Muslims that Akbar had abolished nearly a century earlier.

Aurangzeb also faced a formidable new enemy: Shivaji, who gained control of the Marathas and became the emperor's chief military adversary. The Maratha challenge would persist throughout Aurangzeb's reign and beyond, contributing to the slow weakening of Mughal authority in the Deccan and western India. In 1687, Aurangzeb took the sultanate of Golconda by force — one of the last of the five Deccan sultanates that had resisted Mughal expansion since Akbar's time.

Aurangzeb's long reign — nearly fifty years — was the last period of effective Mughal imperial power. After his death in 1707, the empire fragmented rapidly, weakened by overextension, fiscal strain, and the regional resistance that Aurangzeb's own policies had helped to provoke.

Key Takeaways

  • Shah Jahan's reign was defined by monumental building — the Taj Mahal, Shah Jahanabad, the Peacock Throne — and by an increasing turn toward Islamic orthodoxy after Mumtaz Mahal's death.
  • The Taj Mahal synthesized Central Asian, Persian, and Indian architectural traditions, reflecting the empire's multicultural character.
  • The war of succession (1657–1658) pitted Shah Jahan's sons against one another, with Aurangzeb — the experienced military commander — defeating Dara Shukoh, the court-groomed favorite.
  • Aurangzeb reversed Akbar's religious policies, enforcing strict Islamic principles and reinstating the jiziya tax on non-Muslims.
  • The Maratha challenge under Shivaji became the empire's most persistent military threat, contributing to the long decline of Mughal power after Aurangzeb's death in 1707.
